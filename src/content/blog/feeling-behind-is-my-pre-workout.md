The workouts I'm proudest of did not come from feeling good.

They came from feeling behind. From getting a no. From looking around at people my age and doing the quiet math on how far ahead of me they seem to be. Nobody ever hit a PR because they were content.

I wrote [last time](/post/its-all-mental) about how, past a certain distance, the body stops explaining performance and the mind takes over. This is kind of the other half of that. The mind doesn't just keep me going once I've started. On a lot of days, it's the only reason I start at all. And what it runs on isn't discipline or some clean, Instagram-caption love of the grind. It's a chip on my shoulder. Specifically: feeling behind, and rejection.

Here's how it actually works. There are days where convincing myself to go to the gym feels like negotiating with a toddler. Tired, busy, the couch is right there, I'll go tomorrow. Motivation in the nice sense is gone. Then some thought sneaks in: you're not where you should be, they said no, everyone else is moving and you're standing still. And suddenly I'm putting my shoes on. Not because I feel inspired. Because sitting around with that thought is way more annoying than any set I could do.

That's the trade, really. Training is the one place where feeling behind turns into something I can actually do something about. I can't make someone say yes. I can't speed my life up to match someone else's timeline. But I can add five pounds to the bar. I can run one more kilometre than last week. A no on its own just gets replayed in your head. A no with a barbell attached at least goes somewhere.

And it works, which is the funny part. It works on exactly the days nothing else does.

Which is where it gets a little uncomfortable, because lately I've been asking myself a question I don't love: what is my real motivation?

If the off days are what get me in the door, do I actually like training? Or do I just like proving a point? Is the gym a thing I love, or a thing I use to settle a score with myself?

Psychologists have a framework for this that hit a little close to home. Robert Vallerand and his colleagues split passion into two kinds in a [2003 paper](https://www.intranet.fse.ulaval.ca/fichiers/public/prof/pub_1618__Vallerandetal_2003.pdf): harmonious and obsessive. Harmonious passion is when an activity becomes part of who you are because you freely chose it. Obsessive passion is when it becomes part of who you are because of pressure, usually internal, like needing it to feel like you're enough. Both kinds get people to show up. Both get people to work hard. The difference is what it costs. Harmonious passion tends to come with the good stuff, like actually enjoying it. Obsessive passion is more of a mixed bag, and it's the kind that keeps you going when you probably should stop.

Reading that, I'm pretty sure I'm somewhere in between. Some days I'm there because I love it. Other days I'm there because I've got something to prove. Most days, if I'm honest, it's both.

So is it unhealthy? Maybe a little. If being behind is what gets you moving, you kind of need to keep feeling behind. There's a version of this where you're never allowed to feel caught up, because then what would you train for? That's probably not the best way to live.

But I also don't think it's something I need to fix, because I don't think it's a bug. That's just how I'm wired. Some people are pulled forward by what they want. I seem to get pushed forward by the gap: between where I am and where I think I should be, between the no I got and the yes I'm going to get next time. The push is real. Pretending it isn't would just make me worse at using it.

The goal isn't to stop feeling behind. It's to make sure it points somewhere useful. Feeling behind can send you doom-scrolling at 2am, or it can send you to the gym at 7. Same feeling, different exit.

I'll take the gym.

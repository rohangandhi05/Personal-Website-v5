There is a distance at which the body's contribution to performance asymptotes toward zero.



Most people don't register this until they've either run something absurdly long themselves or watched someone else do it up close. Training, gear, VO2 max: all of that matters early. But somewhere past the halfway point of a real ultramarathon, the physical side quietly runs out of explanatory power, and something else takes over the job of moving the person forward. Call it will, call it identity, call it a narrower kind of stubbornness.

This is the pattern I keep coming back to with ultramarathons, and with endurance sports more broadly: the further a discipline pushes past its ordinary duration, the less the outcome is decided by the body. Physiology carries you through the opening hours. After that you're negotiating with your own mind, and the sport turns into an extended philosophical argument you're having with yourself, in the dark, at altitude, on very little sleep.



Take Badwater 135, which runs through Death Valley in July, where the asphalt gets hot enough to soften the soles of running shoes and the air temperature sits somewhere north of 48 degrees. There is no version of that race where the body is the primary actor. The body is just the vehicle being argued with for 40+ hours while the mind decides whether your will to finish the race is greater than your will to survive.

There's a fairly precise way to locate the moment the body stops accounting for performance. Researchers comparing physiological predictors across ultra distances found that the number of measurable variables correlated with finishing times shrinks as race distance increases, until at 100 kilometers physiology stops explaining outcomes in any meaningful way.

[A separate study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33691287/) of a 65-kilometer trail race found that physiological factors accounted for only about 59 percent of the variance in performance between runners. The remaining 41 percent isn't statistical noise. It's the runner's relationship to their own discomfort, functioning as an unmeasured but decisive variable.

Courtney Dauwalter has become something like a folk theorist of suffering, mostly because of how candidly she talks about wanting it. She describes deliberately entering what she calls the "pain cave," the point where the body can no longer sustain effort and the mind takes over, and trying to expand that threshold a little more each time. This reframes the whole proposition. Suffering isn't an obstacle standing between the athlete and the goal. In ultra-distance events, it's closer to the raw material the goal is built from. You don't train to avoid the wall, you train to have a more useful conversation with it once it shows up.

David Goggins runs on a different psychological fuel than Dauwalter's curiosity about the cave. His version is closer to grievance converted into propulsion, pain treated as proof he's outworking some earlier, weaker version of himself. Same threshold, different reason for walking into it.

Some formats push the absurdity further by removing the finish line entirely. The last one standing style of race, the "backyard ultra" format, has runners complete a loop of a fixed distance every hour, on the hour, forever, until only one person is left who can still start the next lap. There is no course record to chase and no distance to plan around. The race ends when everyone else's mind gives out before yours does. It's less a test of endurance than a test of how long you can keep re-volunteering for the next hour of discomfort, which in my opinion is the purest version of the whole mental argument, stripped of every other variable. 

Cocodona 250 does something stranger to the body: it doesn't just wear it down, it keeps changing the terms. The course runs from low desert heat up into high-altitude cold across the Arizona mountains, so a runner can be fighting heat exhaustion in the morning and hypothermia by nightfall, sometimes within the same twenty-four hours. The body never gets to adapt to one hostile condition before the race swaps in another. The mind ends up doing double duty, managing the discomfort of the moment and constantly recalibrating for whatever discomfort is coming next.

There's a distinction I like that some coaches draw between mental toughness and resilience. Toughness is a single setting: endure and persist, apply more force. Resilience works differently. It's the capacity to adapt, recover, and stay responsive to what's actually happening rather than simply overpowering it. A lot of athletes mistake stubbornness(David Goggin’s mindset) for resilience, defaulting to a "just push through" posture, when the more useful skill is knowing when persistence is warranted and when it isn't. [Addie Bracy](https://endurance-mastery.com/90-percent-of-ultrarunning-is-half-mental/) described resilience as productively engaging with the reality unfolding in front of you, rather than the race you expected to have. It's a fairly stoic idea, restated in the language of trail running.

Then there's the Barkley Marathons, where the actual argument isn't with your legs at all. There's no marked course, no aid stations, no GPS allowed, and the entrants navigate by compass and memory through briar patches and unmarked mountains in Tennessee, tearing pages out of hidden books as proof they found each checkpoint. Most people who fail don't fail because their body quit. They fail because they got lost, or ran out of time doing math on a map at 3 a.m. It's a race that quietly admits the physical part was never really the point and literally challenges ones mental in every possible level.

The motivation research is stranger still. Ultrarunners score lower on boredom susceptibility than most people, lower on pain-related anxiety, higher on risk-seeking, yet lower on disinhibition; they tend to avoid the substances that might dull the experience they're actively pursuing. They aren't running from discomfort; they're running toward the edge of what they can tolerate, mostly to see what's out there. Asked why, marathoners more often cite health, weight, or self-esteem. A majority of surveyed ultrarunners said they'd keep running ultras even after learning it was bad for their health, because the sense of achievement outweighed the health math. That's not a conclusion physiology arrives at. It's a conclusion about what a life is organized around.

None of this is specific to running. The triathlete hits an equivalent wall in the marathon leg, except the body has already been drawn on twice before. The wrestler in the third period isn't really contesting the opponent; they're contesting the impulse suggesting that tapping out would feel like relief. The rower alone with an ergometer has nowhere to hide from that impulse at all, just numbers and breath. Different disciplines produce different shapes of the same demand: the body reaches an honest limit, and everything past that point gets authored by the athlete's relationship to their own mind.

Which is maybe the real reason any of this is worth watching. Not the finish times, not the mountains, not even the suffering itself. It's that somewhere out there, right now, someone is deciding, alone, in the dark, with no one keeping score, whether they still want to be the kind of person who keeps going. Nobody's checking. Nobody would know if they stopped. They keep going anyway.

That's the whole sport, really. Everything else is just scenery.

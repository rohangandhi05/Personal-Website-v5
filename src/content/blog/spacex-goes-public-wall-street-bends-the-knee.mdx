Today we got the SpaceX IPO, and if you somehow missed it, congratulations on being mentally well. We got the world's first trillionaire, the biggest IPO in history, and the world's biggest banks doing free work just to stay close to Elon Musk. Times Square's New Year's Eve ball rose and glowed Mars red. SpaceX employees are eating logo-branded steaks tonight on the 57th floor of JPMorgan's building, and Musk orchestrated the whole thing not from Wall Street, but from the city he built himself: Starbase, Texas, the Disney World of Tesla, branded as the gateway to Mars.



Here's what actually happened the day SpaceX went public, and why you could call it Musk's coronation.



The Numbers



The stock opened at $150, which is $15 above the $135 IPO price. It hit $176 during the day and closed at $161, up 19%. SpaceX is now worth $2.1 trillion, the sixth most valuable company in America. Elon Musk is the first person in history worth over $1 trillion. He's a man one comma away from a country's GDP.



The demand was a wave like nothing we've seen before. $350 billion in total orders chased just $75 billion in shares. We haven't seen demand curves like this since MSG Knicks finals tickets. Over $100 billion of that demand came from retail investors alone, and for what it's worth, most of them didn't get what they wanted: the group got about $15 billion in stock total. One third of firms that placed orders got zero shares, because in an oversubscribed IPO, asking for stock does not mean you get it. SpaceX and its bankers got to choose who made it onto the cap table. Everyone was online, but not everyone got in.



Robinhood crashed, obviously, in the first minutes of trading from record-breaking traffic. SpaceX was the most-bought stock by retail investors today by far, with 3.5 times more net purchases than Nvidia, which came in second for the first time.



The Mars Father



Musk power-played this deal the whole way through. Every move he made set the tone: he's the Mars father, and you come to him, not the other way around. He didn't go to New York. He stayed in Starbase, Texas, behind a Nasdaq-branded podium in the desert. His president, Gwynne Shotwell, who actually runs the company day to day, rang the opening bell in New York alongside SpaceX's CFO and, of course, Musk's mother, May. An hour before the market opened, SpaceX launched 29 Starlink satellites from Cape Canaveral, like fireworks on a holiday, except the fireworks were an actual rocket used as a flex. Only SpaceX.



There were more power moves on Wall Street. SpaceX negotiated its total fees down to $500 million, which isn't even 1% of the $75 billion raise. About 0.67%, to be exact. Normal IPO fees run 4 to 7 percent, but Musk got the friends and family deal.



Then there was the green shoe. Basically, it's what Wall Street calls an IPO's "sell extra shares" button: if demand is strong enough, banks can sell up to 15% more stock after the IPO. For SpaceX, that meant another $11.2525 billion in shares. Normally banks get paid fees on that too, but SpaceX negotiated the green shoe fee down to zero, meaning the banks got the privilege of placing another $11 billion of SpaceX stock without collecting any cut. That's how badly Wall Street titans wanted to be attached to this deal.



To show just how whipped they are, Musk also made senior Morgan Stanley execs, including CEO Ted Pick, literally wear Hulk-green shoes on the trading floor, a flex demonstrating exactly what kind of leverage he holds. Musk's message: you're on my planet now. Which honestly feels like a page out of Trump's Florsheim playbook.



The Party



After the market closed, the real celebration started. Times Square joined in: the New Year's Eve ball, the one that drops every December 31st, rose above One Times Square and glowed Mars red to mark SpaceX's debut and Musk's vision of sending humans to the red planet. It was cinematic, the symbol of Earth's biggest New Year's party lighting up for a company trying to make Mars its next destination.



JPMorgan hosted the closing party on the 57th floor of its new headquarters, complete with tomahawk steaks branded with SpaceX and JPMorgan logos, in a display of opulence befitting Louis XIV. Cocktails were named "The Future Is Public" and "Falcon 9." Apparently, Jamie Dimon personally pitched the party to Musk during a meeting in Texas. This is an IPO so big that Jamie Dimon is working as the event planner.



The wooing this man got from the biggest names on Wall Street is frankly unreal, especially given the history. Musk testified under oath in 2023 that, quote, "JPMorgan hates Tesla," in a nutshell. Three years later, Jamie Dimon came to Musk's throne room to bend the knee, and now JPMorgan is lighting up its building with SpaceX rockets and carving logos into steaks for him. Turns out there are no hard feelings when the biggest IPO in history is on the table.



The Underdog Story



Musk gave a genuinely inspiring underdog speech. He said that when he started SpaceX, he gave it less than a 10% chance of succeeding at all. His words: "I told people, look, we're probably going to fail, but we should give it a try, because if we don't, we will never be a truly space-faring civilization." Clearly, he didn't feel comfortable leaving that job to Jeff Bezos. Love him or hate him, he's had a pretty good 24-year run with SpaceX.



There are some genuinely good human stories from today too. A Canadian teachers' pension fund invested early and is now sitting on an $11 billion windfall. The New York Times profiled an employee who spent 15 years amassing SpaceX shares and is now looking at a payday most lottery winners would kill for.



Some analysts are calling the mismatch between SpaceX's current revenue and its valuation an absurd stretch, pricing the company at 94 times revenue. Other investors think betting against the Musk premium is always a losing trade. We'll see which side has the better crystal ball.



What It Means Beyond SpaceX



The bigger question is what this means beyond SpaceX itself. This wasn't just an IPO. This was Wall Street answering the question of how much investors are willing to pay for the companies building the next version of the world.



SpaceX isn't a pure AI company the way OpenAI and Anthropic are, but it belongs to the same market dream. The company lost $5 billion last year and priced at a valuation that would have sounded delusional until very recently, arguably still does, and the stock went up anyway. That matters for OpenAI and Anthropic, because both are heading toward the same question: can you ask public investors to pay not for what a company is today, but for the future it says it's building? SpaceX just proved the answer can be yes, though it's worth remembering this is Elon, who played Wall Street like a master and got its lords and vassals to wear green sneakers for him.



None of this removes the uncertainty. It just kicks off a new round of questions. Will the price hold? Employees can start selling shares after Q2 earnings this summer, with more selling windows opening every few weeks through December. Over 20 SpaceX-linked ETFs have already been filed, the start of a whole SpaceX economy within Wall Street. Whether the price survives the selling pressure is the next chapter.



What's certain is that today, it's a very good day to be Elon.

Martial artists are often slow to see how their own beliefs about violence get warped, by tradition, and by a natural desire to avoid getting hurt in training. You can spend a lifetime mastering an ancient fighting system, and build a following of students who spend years trying to copy you, without ever finding out you can't actually defend yourself. Delusions of martial prowess work a lot like religious faith. But there's one big difference: a person of faith can always explain away a contradiction between belief and reality. An inability to fight can't be explained away. It's easy to spot, and once it's exposed, there's no talking your way out of it.

The strangest case I know of is Yanagi Ryuken, a self-styled aikido master who believed he could defeat multiple attackers without ever touching them, relying instead on the "magic" power of chi. Video of him demonstrating this shows students flying across the room, seemingly untouched. It's hard to know what those students actually believed was happening to them. Watching the “sport” of aikido is almost comedic in itself; no offence, reminds of 2010’s Bollywood films. Don’t believe me, give it a watch yourself.

Milder versions of this same self-deception show up in most martial arts, because almost all training involves some degree of cooperation from your partner. Students trade choreographed attacks, in a set order, resetting between reps. That stop-start rhythm is fine for learning a new move, but taken too far, it turns into a kind of pantomime that doesn't prepare anyone for a real fight.

There's a second problem: a lot of techniques are too dangerous to practice at full speed, things like eye gouges and groin strikes. So students are left to just assume these moves would end a fight instantly if they ever "had to" use them. Real-world reports suggest that assumption doesn't always hold up.

This is where Brazilian jiu-jitsu stands apart from almost everything else. BJJ can be trained at 100 percent resistance, safely, which means you can actually find out whether your technique works, no assuming required. Striking arts can also be trained at full resistance, but not safely, because getting hit in the head repeatedly is bad for you. And even at full intensity, striking keeps a layer of uncertainty that never fully goes away: even a professional boxer can't say for certain what happens when his fist meets someone's jaw. The guy might drop. He might not. The boxer might break his own hand. BJJ doesn't have that problem. Even a beginner knows exactly what happens when a triangle chokes is locked in correctly. In grappling, theory and reality line up completely.

I can tell you from experience: rolling with someone who actually knows BJJ feels like falling into deep water when you can't swim. You thrash. You lose. And once you learn to swim, it's hard to even remember what the problem was: why couldn't you just relax and float? BJJ recreates that gap between panic and competence over and over. Training is a cycle of being "drowned" in clever new ways, and then being taught, again and again, how to swim.

It doesn't matter if you're a lifelong boxer, a karate black belt, or just a genuinely tough person: step onto the mat with a BJJ black belt and you'll relearn humility fast. Few things are as jarring as being calmly controlled by someone your size or smaller, and tapped out by a choke or joint lock no matter how hard you resist. A few minutes of that, and most people are hooked. They want to learn whatever this person knows. Then comes the payoff: you're shown how to escape the traps just sprung on you, and eventually how to set them yourself. That cycle, get caught, learn the escape, catch someone else, is what makes BJJ so addictive. I have yet to find anything similar to BJJ - leaving you feeling so humble yet hungry to understand.

### Why BJJ Won the Question Everyone Was Asking

Most people who've followed martial arts have known about BJJ since 1993, when the UFC put it on the map. The UFC was the first big mixed martial arts event to pit different styles against each other directly: boxing, wrestling, karate, Muay Thai, judo, sambo, kung fu, and more, matched up one-on-one with almost no rules. No weight classes, no rounds, barely any judges. Fights ended by knockout, submission, or a towel thrown in.

A lot of people found it brutal to watch. But to martial artists, it was a science experiment two thousand years in the making, finally answering the one question fighters had always argued about: what actually works? The answer surprised almost everyone. It wasn't boxing, wrestling, or karate. A comparatively small Brazilian jiu-jitsu fighter named Royce Gracie kept beating bigger, stronger, more decorated opponents, over and over.

That result has picked up some caveats since, but decades of testing have only confirmed the core lesson. Take away the rules, and fighters instinctively grab hold of each other and end up grappling. In boxing or kickboxing, referees hide this by constantly separating fighters. In a real fight, nobody separates you, and once you're in that clinch, the bigger or more experienced grappler usually takes it to the ground. And once a fight's on the ground, there's no substitute for knowing jiu-jitsu.

Today almost every serious MMA fighter has absorbed this lesson, so a BJJ background alone isn't the advantage it used to be: everyone grapples now. But the underlying truth hasn't changed, and it shapes how fighters train at every range:

At arm's length, you want to strike like a boxer and kick like a Thai fighter.

Closer in, you want elbows and knees like a Thai fighter.

In the clinch, you want wrestling: controlling posture, throwing your opponent, or stopping him from throwing you.

And on the ground, BJJ is still the answer. The one update is that since everyone now grapples, the ability to strike effectively from the ground has become more important too. You don't have to call it "BJJ" specifically (sambo and submission wrestling teach a lot of the same skills), but no art has mapped out ground fighting as thoroughly.

### Where BJJ Falls Short for Self-Defense

There's one habit BJJ can teach you that works against you outside the gym: because it's so effective on the ground, it's easy to start treating the ground as the goal. When you're used to drilling armbars and chokes, it's easy to forget that in an actual altercation, your opponent might be punching you the whole time, might have a weapon, or might have friends ready to kick you in the head while you're down there. Years of ground-fighting practice can quietly erase the more basic truth: if a fight breaks out, the ground is the last place you want to be.

Still, training BJJ for self-defense means preparing for the worst case, and the worst case is more likely than you'd think. If someone bigger and stronger ever attacks you, there's a real chance you'll end up wrestling on the ground whether you intend to or not. Knowing what to do in that moment, instead of just relying on raw instinct, is one of the biggest gaps between competence and helplessness I've ever seen.

The AI story that feels most like a fever dream to me isn't happening in a lab. It's happening in the San Francisco housing market, where people are so intoxicated with AI money that they're buying houses with stock they can't technically sell yet.

A guy listed his home for $3 million and slipped one cursed little sentence into the listing: he'd take payment in Anthropic or OpenAI stock. Which, these days, is all the rage in San Francisco real estate listings. And of course it worked like breadcrumbs to pigeons. Within days, two OpenAI employees showed up to his open house like it was a pitch contest. Worth noting: neither company is even public yet, so the money on the table is still technically imaginary, or at least illiquid. If the San Francisco housing market is a nightmare right now, the AI industry is its boogeyman.

The Wall Street Journal ran a piece on how one guy's house basically encapsulates everything broken about the AI economy and the real estate market at once, and it's a lot.

The seller is a real estate developer who listed his three-bedroom last month for just under $3 million: 10-foot ceilings, marble kitchen, remote-control skylights, the kind of place that would do fine by Architectural Digest standards. Then he added the fateful clause: he'd accept shares of OpenAI or Anthropic as payment. Right now, those shares might be the most valuable currency in the world.

This guy was really using the house as a strategy to acquire pre-IPO equity. He looked at $3 million, actual, legal-tender United States currency, and decided he could get something even more valuable than that. The house wasn't the asset. The house was a boring middleman he was using as a coupon to redeem for Frontier Lab shares.

Both OpenAI and Anthropic are still private companies, so by asking for stock instead of cash, he narrowed his buyer pool dramatically, down to the small, currently ultra-powerful class of citizens who happen to be OpenAI or Anthropic employees, or investors sitting on the golden eggs of pre-IPO stock. And like clockwork, that's exactly who showed up.

OpenAI employees materialized almost instantly, shares in hand. One buyer bid over a million dollars above asking, but paid in his own OpenAI shares, which he seemed to be marking up to whatever price made his bid look good. Which is kind of like offering to overpay for a house in Pokémon cards, and insisting that one of your cards is worth exactly whatever number makes the house affordable for you.

The second buyer got cold feet for the most San Francisco reason in human history: OpenAI filed to go public, and he decided he'd rather keep the stock than trade it for something as pedestrian as shelter. House versus stock. The piece of paper won. This is happening all over the city, before either company has sold a single share to the public.

One listing went viral after someone at a firm invested in both OpenAI and Anthropic (not exactly a neutral party) leaked it online. Another guy listed his wine country vacation home in Sonoma and offered half a million dollars off if you paid in Anthropic stock. All of this hype is probably inflating the valuation of these stocks well before they've actually gone public. We haven't seen a rush to an entrance like this since Beatles concerts, or Black Friday at Walmart.

The reason OpenAI and Anthropic employees would even consider trading stock for a house: there are fewer than 600 homes for sale in all of San Francisco right now, about 40% below normal. Basically nobody is selling unless they can get a bite of the boom, because there's a billionaire around every corner and every homeowner in the city knows it. It's an amazing seller's market, and the stock-friendly listings are doing the trick. Everybody with AI money is circling them, and prices have gone completely insane.

But this isn't funny for everyday people. The article notes one couple, an engineer and a tech sales worker, both solid high earners, whose landlord heard through the grapevine that everyone was getting rich and decided to sell the apartment out from under them to cash in. Now they're house hunting whether they like it or not. They bid $600,000 over asking on one place and lost. It went for almost a million over.

Now imagine everyone else in that city who doesn't have stock options: teachers, nurses, people who've rented the same apartment for a decade and just got the text from their landlord saying "I'm selling." They're not in the bidding war. They can't be. They're just standing in the blast radius, watching people pay for houses with maybe the most valuable monopoly money in history.

That's the AI boom, and it hasn't even technically started.

I will say, renting in New York suddenly looks like bargain hunting. But then again, Anthropic is opening a big new office in New York. So buckle up.

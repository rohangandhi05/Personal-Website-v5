In 2008, Airbnb was broke, so the founders started selling cereal.

Specifically, election-themed cereal boxes: "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's," which they designed and sold for \$40 a box. It raised about \$30,000 and kept the company alive. Y Combinator's Paul Graham later said the cereal stunt was a big part of why he took a chance on them.

The idea itself started a year earlier, when designers Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia couldn't pay rent in San Francisco, so they put air mattresses on their floor and rented them to people attending a design conference. "AirBed & Breakfast." Nathan Blecharczyk joined to build the site.

How it makes money: service fees. When you book a stay, Airbnb charges the guest a fee and takes a cut from the host. It never owns a single home, which means it makes money from the world's largest hotel chain without owning any hotels.

Q2 2026: \$3.6 billion in revenue, up 17%, on \$27.2 billion of bookings.

The 2026 twist: Airbnb is now letting actual hotels on the platform (boutique and independent ones), plus "experiences" like cooking classes and tours, and "services" like private chefs, grocery delivery and airport pickups. Chesky's ambition: "an Amazon for services, at least for traveling and living."

The irony: the company that disrupted hotels now lists hotels.

Public (NASDAQ: ABNB). Started with air mattresses and cereal. Still a balanced breakfast.

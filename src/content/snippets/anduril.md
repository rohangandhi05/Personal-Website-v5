Anduril is named after Aragorn's sword in The Lord of the Rings: the blade reforged to fight for the West. Subtle, these people are not.

The founder is Palmer Luckey, the guy who invented the Oculus Rift in his parents' garage, sold it to Facebook for \$2 billion, got pushed out, and decided his next act would be building weapons. In Hawaiian shirts. Co-founders include alumni of Palantir and Founders Fund, which explains the Tolkien branding. They all have it.

What it builds: autonomous drones, counter-drone systems, underwater vehicles, surveillance towers along borders, and Lattice, the AI software that ties it all together so one person can command a swarm.

How it makes money: selling those products to the U.S. military and allies. The twist is the business model. Old-school defense contractors mostly get paid "cost-plus," meaning the government covers their costs and adds a profit margin, which rewards going slow and over budget. Anduril spends its own money building the product first, then sells it, like a normal tech company. The Pentagon likes it. The incumbents don't.

The big one is Arsenal-1, a nearly \$1 billion factory in Ohio. In July it rolled out its first Fury, an AI-piloted fighter drone built to fly alongside crewed jets for the Air Force.

Revenue more than doubled to \$2.2 billion in 2025. In May it raised \$5 billion at a \$61 billion valuation, led by Thrive and a16z, double its valuation from eleven months earlier.

Private. The sword was broken. It has been reforged. It has a Series H.

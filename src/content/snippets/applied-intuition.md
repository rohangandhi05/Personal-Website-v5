Before a self-driving car is allowed anywhere near your street, it has to survive millions of miles of chaos: kids chasing balls into the road, deer at night, a mattress falling off a pickup truck, a guy in a chicken costume crossing against the light.

You can't wait around for all of that to happen in real life. So you build a video game where it happens on purpose, over and over, and you let the car's software practice in there.

That was the original idea behind Applied Intuition, one of the most valuable startups most people have never heard of.

It was founded in 2017 by Qasar Younis, the former chief operating officer of Y Combinator, and Peter Ludwig, an engineer who'd worked at Google. Its simulation software became a standard tool for many of the world's biggest carmakers.

How it makes money: software licenses and contracts with automakers and truck makers. It's since grown beyond simulation into the software that actually runs inside vehicles, the operating system and driver-assistance features, so carmakers don't have to build all of it themselves.

The big pivot: **defense.** The same autonomy software that drives a sedan can drive a military truck, a tractor or a drone. Applied now works with the U.S. Army and Navy on autonomous vehicles and is pushing hard into military systems.

It was valued at \$15 billion in June 2025, and in November 2026 it was reported to be in talks to raise at about \$30 billion, led by General Catalyst.

Private. It started with a driving game. Now it's building the driver.

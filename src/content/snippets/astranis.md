Traditional communications satellites are school-bus-sized giants that cost hundreds of millions of dollars, take years to build, and get shared by lots of customers at once. Like renting a room in a huge apartment building in the sky.

Astranis builds the opposite: small satellites, a fraction of the size, that sit in geostationary orbit about 36,000 km up, hovering over one spot on Earth. It calls them MicroGEOs. The big difference: **one customer can own a whole satellite**, aimed right at their country or region.

Who wants their own satellite? Places the internet forgot. Remote parts of Alaska, where Astranis's first satellite was built to roughly triple the bandwidth available to rural communities. Mountain villages in Peru. Islands, ships and countries that don't want to rely on someone else's network.

How it makes money: selling or leasing whole satellites, plus the bandwidth they provide, to telecom companies and governments. Because the satellites are smaller and mass-produced, Astranis can build them faster and cheaper than the giants, and launch several at once on a single rocket. It reported more than \$1 billion in signed contracts.

It was founded in 2015 by John Gedmark and Ryan McLinko and builds its satellites in San Francisco. It has five MicroGEOs in orbit and wants 100 by 2030.

The obvious question: what about Starlink? Starlink uses thousands of satellites close to Earth. Astranis bets that some customers would rather own their own dedicated piece of the sky than share Elon's.

In May 2026 it raised \$300 million at about a \$2.8 billion valuation.

Private. Some people buy a vacation home. Some countries buy a satellite.

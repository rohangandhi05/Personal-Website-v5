In 1981, Salomon Brothers pushed out one of its partners, a guy named Michael Bloomberg, with a payout of about \$10 million.

He used it to build a computer.

That computer became the **Bloomberg Terminal**: a two-screen setup with a chunky keyboard full of colored keys, sitting on the desk of basically every trader, banker and fund manager in the world. Every stock price, bond, currency and commodity, live. Every piece of financial news. Charts, analytics and data you can't easily get anywhere else.

How it makes money: renting those terminals. One reportedly costs about \$32,000 a year, and there are over 300,000 subscribers. The terminal is estimated to be around three quarters of Bloomberg's revenue, which outside estimates put near \$15 billion a year. Nobody knows exactly, because Bloomberg is private and doesn't publish its numbers.

The thing most people don't know: a huge part of why Wall Street can't quit the terminal isn't the data. It's the **chat**. Instant Bloomberg, the messaging system built into the terminal, is where traders talk to each other, negotiate deals and trade gossip. Canceling your terminal means leaving the group chat. Nobody wants to leave the group chat.

The first customer was Merrill Lynch, which bought 20 terminals and took a stake in the company. Bloomberg later bought it back.

Then the terminal money built an empire: Bloomberg News, Bloomberg TV, Businessweek, and reporters like Joe Weisenthal and Mark Gurman, both recent TBPN guests. Michael Bloomberg, meanwhile, became a three-term mayor of New York City and ran for president.

Private. He still owns an estimated 88% of the company and has pledged his stake to Bloomberg Philanthropies. The terminal pays for a lot of good causes.

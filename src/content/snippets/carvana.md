Carvana built giant glass towers full of cars and called them vending machines.

You buy a used car online, show up at the tower, put a big novelty coin in a slot, and your car rolls out of the building like a bag of chips. It is the most fun anyone has ever had at a car dealership, because it isn't one.

What it does: sells used cars entirely online. You browse, finance and buy on your phone, then Carvana delivers it to your driveway or the vending machine. You get seven days to return it if you hate it. No salesperson saying "let me talk to my manager."

How it makes money, three ways:
1. **The car itself:** profit on each vehicle it buys, reconditions and resells.
2. **The loan:** most buyers finance through Carvana, which makes the loans and then sells them to investors.
3. **Wholesale:** it owns ADESA, a big car auction business, for cars that don't make the retail cut.

The comeback story is the wild part. In 2022, as interest rates spiked and used car prices fell, Carvana's stock dropped about 99%. Bankruptcy jokes everywhere. Then it cut costs hard, restructured its debt, and came roaring back.

In 2025 it sold 596,641 cars, up 43%, for \$20.3 billion in revenue. In the April–June 2026 quarter it made a record \$513 million in profit. The long-term goal: 3 million cars a year.

Founder and CEO Ernie Garcia III started it in 2012.

Public (NYSE: CVNA). Went from almost zero to a quarterly profit record. Not a lemon.

Tale of the tape.

In the blue corner: a normal AI chip. Cut from a silicon wafer about the size of a dinner plate, along with dozens of its siblings. Fits in your palm.

In the red corner: Cerebras. Looked at that dinner plate and said "what if we just didn't cut it."

That's the whole idea. The Wafer-Scale Engine 3 is one chip the size of the entire wafer: 4 trillion transistors and 900,000 cores. Cerebras says it's 57 times bigger than Nvidia's H100. The payoff is speed. Data doesn't have to hop between separate chips, so AI models answer absurdly fast. Fast enough that OpenAI signed a deal worth more than \$10 billion.

How it makes money: selling its CS-3 systems to labs and governments, and more and more by renting its chips in the cloud and charging per token, basically a meter running every time a model types a word.

The thing most people missed: when Cerebras first filed to go public in 2024, its paperwork showed that one customer, the Abu Dhabi AI firm G42, made up 87% of its revenue for the first half of that year. One whale. Wall Street got nervous, the IPO stalled, and Cerebras spent the next stretch signing customers like AWS and OpenAI.

It worked. In May 2026 Cerebras listed on Nasdaq at \$185 a share, raising \$5.55 billion in the biggest U.S. IPO of the year so far.

CEO Andrew Feldman has done this before: he sold his last chip startup, SeaMicro, to AMD.

Public (NASDAQ: CBRS). Early backer: Benchmark. Winner by decision: not cutting the pizza.

In the lobby of Cloudflare's San Francisco office there's a wall of about 100 lava lamps.

It's not decor. A camera films them, and because the blobs move in truly unpredictable ways, the footage is turned into random numbers that help encrypt internet traffic. Part of the internet's security runs on a 1960s hippie lamp. Groovy.

What Cloudflare actually does: it sits between websites and the people visiting them. A huge share of the web runs through Cloudflare, which speeds sites up by caching them near you, and blocks attacks before they reach the site. That's why when Cloudflare has a bad day, half your apps go down at once and the whole internet briefly learns what Cloudflare is.

How it makes money: freemium. Millions of small sites use it for free. Businesses pay monthly plans, and big companies sign large enterprise contracts for security, networking and developer tools. The free users are the bait and the training data: seeing that much traffic makes its security better for the paying customers.

The newest fight: AI bots. In 2025 Cloudflare started blocking AI crawlers by default for its customers and launched "pay per crawl," letting websites charge AI companies to scrape their pages. In 2026 it's pushing further toward paying publishers when their content actually shows up in an AI answer. Cloudflare has made itself the toll collector between AI and the content AI eats.

Public (NYSE: NET). Co-founder and CEO Matthew Prince. Securing the internet, one blob at a time.

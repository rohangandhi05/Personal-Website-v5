CoreWeave started as a crypto mining operation.

Its founders, Michael Intrator, Brian Venturo and Brannin McBee, were commodities traders who bought a pile of Nvidia graphics cards around 2017 to mine Ethereum. Then crypto crashed, and they realized the GPUs were more valuable doing something else: computing for other people.

Good call. Those GPUs turned out to be the most valuable hardware in the world.

What CoreWeave is now: a "neocloud." Like Amazon Web Services, but specialized in one thing, renting out huge clusters of Nvidia GPUs to train and run AI. Customers include OpenAI, Meta and Microsoft. In fact, when CoreWeave went public in March 2025, its paperwork showed Microsoft alone made up about 62% of its 2024 revenue.

How it makes money: long-term contracts. AI companies sign multi-year deals to rent GPU capacity. CoreWeave buys chips, builds or leases data centers, and charges for the computing power.

The numbers are enormous. 2025 revenue was \$5.1 billion, up 168%, with \$12–13 billion expected in 2026. Its backlog of signed contracts hit about \$104 billion by mid-2026, including a \$21 billion deal with Meta.

The scary part: it's built on debt. To buy all those GPUs, CoreWeave borrowed heavily, with debt passing \$17 billion by early 2026. If AI demand cools, that's a problem. If it doesn't, it's a money printer. Wall Street has been arguing about which one every quarter.

Nvidia is both an investor and its supplier.

July 19, 2024. You wake up. Your flight is cancelled. The airport screens are blue. So are the hospital's, the bank's and the TV station's.

That was CrowdStrike. A single faulty update to its security software crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers around the world in one morning. It's widely called the largest IT outage in history. Delta alone cancelled thousands of flights and sued.

So why is CrowdStrike in a list of thriving companies?

Because it came back.

What it does: CrowdStrike's Falcon software sits on computers and servers and watches for hackers, malware and ransomware in real time. It's the security guard that lives inside your laptop. CrowdStrike is also the firm that investigated the 2016 hack of the Democratic National Committee.

How it makes money: subscriptions, charged per device and per module. Companies pay every year to keep the guard on duty.

After the outage, analysts expected customers to walk. Instead CrowdStrike offered discounts and extra products, poured money into reliability, and kept almost everyone. In fiscal 2026 it added more than \$1 billion in new recurring revenue for the first time. By July 2026, annual recurring revenue was \$5.84 billion, up 25%.

The irony nobody forgets: co-founder George Kurtz was CTO of McAfee in 2010, when a bad McAfee update crashed huge numbers of Windows PCs. Lightning struck twice, and he was standing near the tree both times.

Public (NASDAQ: CRWD). Turned it off and on again. It worked.

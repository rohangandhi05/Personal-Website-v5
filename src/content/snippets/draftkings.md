If you watched any sports in 2015, you remember the DraftKings ads. All of them. Every commercial break. It was less an ad campaign and more an occupation.

Back then DraftKings was a daily fantasy sports company: pick a lineup of players for one night, win cash. Then in 2018 the Supreme Court struck down the federal ban on sports betting, letting each state decide for itself. DraftKings sprinted through the door.

Today it runs a legal online sportsbook in 26 states plus Washington, DC, plus online casino games in some states.

How it makes money: the hold. When you bet, DraftKings sets the odds so that across millions of bets, it keeps a slice. For the 2025–26 NFL season it said its sportsbook hold hit 16%. It also spends heavily on promotions ("\$200 in bonus bets!") to get you in the door, then hopes you stay.

The big 2026 twist: prediction markets. Companies like Kalshi let people trade contracts on sports outcomes in states where sports betting is still illegal, because those contracts are regulated federally as financial products, not gambling. DraftKings could either fight it or join it. It joined. DraftKings Predictions launched in 38 states, and it's building its own exchange.

So the company that was once fighting for sports betting to be legal is now offering something that technically isn't sports betting, in states where sports betting isn't legal. Lawyers are having a great year.

2026 revenue guidance: \$6.5 to \$6.9 billion.

Public (NASDAQ: DKNG). The house always wins. It's just finding new houses.

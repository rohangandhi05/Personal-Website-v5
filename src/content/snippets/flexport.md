October 2021. Supply chains are melted. Dozens of container ships are floating off Los Angeles waiting to unload, and nobody can get a PlayStation, a couch or a bike.

Ryan Petersen, the CEO of Flexport, rents a boat, tours the port, and posts a long Twitter thread explaining exactly where the bottleneck is: there's nowhere to stack empty containers. Within days, local officials temporarily relax the container stacking rules. A startup CEO's tweet thread moved the port of Long Beach.

That's Flexport: a freight forwarder with a Twitter account and a lot of software.

What it does: when a company needs to get products from a factory in China or Vietnam to a warehouse in Ohio, Flexport handles all of it. Booking space on ships and planes, trucks, customs paperwork, tariffs, warehousing. Traditionally this industry ran on fax machines, phone calls and spreadsheets. Flexport put it in a dashboard.

How it makes money: fees and margins on every shipment it moves, plus customs brokerage and logistics services. Revenue was estimated at about \$2.1 billion in 2024.

Before Flexport, Petersen ran ImportGenius, a site that tracked import records, and before that he imported scooters and other goods from China. He's lived the pain he's selling the cure for.

The 2026 twist: in February the Supreme Court struck down a big set of tariffs, and companies suddenly could claim refunds on what they'd paid. Flexport built AI tools to find and file those refunds for customers, and its ocean volumes have been surging.

Private. Backed by Founders Fund and others. Making the boats go faster, one thread at a time.

Look up at the next intersection you drive through. There's a decent chance you'll spot a small black camera on a pole with a little solar panel on top.

That's probably a Flock camera. And it probably just read your license plate.

Flock Safety was founded in 2017 in Atlanta by Garrett Langley after cars in his neighborhood kept getting broken into and nobody could figure out who did it. His idea: cheap, solar-powered cameras that read license plates and vehicle details, so neighborhoods and police could look up a car connected to a crime. It went through Y Combinator.

How it makes money: subscriptions. Cities, police departments, homeowners associations, schools and stores pay a yearly fee for each camera, plus software to search the footage. It's also expanded into gunshot detection and police drones.

It grew fast because police love it: it's cheap compared to traditional systems, and it does help solve crimes like car thefts and kidnappings. In 2026 it was valued at around \$8.3 billion.

The backlash grew fast too. Privacy groups argue it's building a nationwide surveillance network of where every car goes, and there have been documented cases of officers misusing the system, including using it to track people they knew personally. Questions about data being shared with other agencies, including for immigration enforcement, made it worse. More than 50 cities and towns have moved to cancel or switch off their Flock contracts this year, according to activist trackers.

Private. It's either solving crime or watching everyone. The debate is whether it's both.

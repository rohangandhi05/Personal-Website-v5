During World War II, a single Ford plant in Michigan built a B-24 bomber roughly every hour. Today, the U.S. defense industry struggles to build enough missiles to keep up with demand.

Hadrian's founder, Chris Power, an Australian engineer, thinks a big part of the problem is surprisingly unglamorous: **the small metal parts.** Every rocket, satellite, jet and missile is made of thousands of precisely machined components. Those are mostly built in small, old machine shops run by a workforce that's retiring faster than it's being replaced.

Hadrian's answer: highly automated factories that make those precision parts with software and robots doing much of the work humans used to do by hand. Its software system, Opus, runs the factory floor, and Hadrian says it can get a new factory up and running in under six months.

How it makes money: it's a contract manufacturer. Aerospace and defense companies send Hadrian designs, and Hadrian sells them the finished parts. More factories, more parts, more revenue. The pitch to customers is faster delivery, and to the Pentagon, a U.S. industrial base that can actually scale.

Founded in 2020 in Los Angeles, it has factories in Torrance, California, and Arizona.

Defense demand has exploded, and investors noticed. In January 2026 Hadrian was valued at \$1.6 billion. In August it raised \$1.37 billion at a \$7.87 billion valuation, almost five times higher, to build more factories.

Private. Named after the Roman emperor famous for building a wall. This Hadrian builds the parts that go into everything else.

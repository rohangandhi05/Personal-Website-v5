There's an old joke in physics: nuclear fusion is the energy of the future, and it always will be. For about 70 years, working fusion power has been "30 years away."

Helion is trying to end the joke. On a deadline. With a penalty clause.

Fusion is what powers the sun: smashing tiny atoms together so they fuse and release huge amounts of energy, with no carbon emissions and far less long-lived waste than today's nuclear plants. The problem is doing it on Earth and getting more energy out than you put in.

Helion's approach is different from the giant donut-shaped reactors you might have seen. It fires plasma from both ends of a long machine, slams it together in the middle and compresses it with powerful magnets. And instead of using the heat to boil water and spin a turbine like almost every other power plant, Helion aims to pull electricity directly out of the magnetic field.

How it makes money: right now, it doesn't. But in 2023 it signed the first fusion power purchase agreement ever, with **Microsoft**: Helion promised to deliver 50 megawatts of electricity from a plant called Orion, in Malaga, Washington, with power starting in 2028 and financial penalties if it doesn't.

Construction on Orion started in July 2025. In February 2026, its Polaris prototype hit plasma temperatures of 150 million degrees Celsius, about ten times hotter than the center of the sun.

Sam Altman is Helion's chairman and one of its biggest investors.

Private. The countdown is real. 2028 is no longer "30 years away."

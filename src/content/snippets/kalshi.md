On Kalshi, you can bet on whether it'll rain in New York tomorrow, who wins the Super Bowl, what the inflation number will be, or who wins an election.

Except, Kalshi will tell you, it's not betting. It's trading.

Here's the trick. Kalshi is regulated by the CFTC, the federal agency that oversees futures markets for things like oil and corn. Each question is an "event contract" priced from 1 to 99 cents, which reads as the market's odds. If you buy "Yes" at 60 cents and it happens, you get \$1. Because it's a federally regulated financial exchange, Kalshi argues state gambling laws don't apply.

How it makes money: a small fee on trades. It doesn't care who wins, because it isn't the house. It's the exchange, matching buyers with sellers.

The founders: Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara, MIT grads who met in school. Lopes Lara trained as a ballerina before switching to engineering. They spent years fighting regulators, including suing the CFTC in 2024 to win the right to list election contracts. They won.

Now it's huge. Sports are the majority of the volume. Kalshi said its annualized trading volume hit \$178 billion, and it was valued at \$22 billion in May, with reports of a round at around \$40 billion after that.

The fight: many states say sports contracts are just unlicensed sports betting. Arizona and Utah have filed criminal charges, some courts have blocked it, others haven't, and the question of who regulates prediction markets looks headed for the Supreme Court.

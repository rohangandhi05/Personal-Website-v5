Your uncle thinks Nvidia makes graphics cards for Call of Duty.

Your uncle is about 6% right.

In the quarter ending January 2026, gaming brought in \$3.7 billion. The data center business, meaning the chips that train and run every AI model you've heard of, brought in \$62.3 billion. Nvidia is a gaming company the way McDonald's is a toy company because of the Happy Meal.

What most people don't know:

**It started at a Denny's.** Three engineers planned the company over coffee at a Denny's in San Jose in 1993. There's a plaque in the booth now.

**It almost died early.** Its first chip flopped so badly that "our company is 30 days from going out of business" became an unofficial company motto. They kept saying it for years after they were fine.

**The real moat is software.** In 2006 Nvidia released CUDA, a free toolkit that let programmers use graphics chips for regular math. Nobody cared for a decade. Then AI arrived, every researcher already knew CUDA, and switching to a rival chip meant rewriting everything. Free software, very expensive lock-in.

**It sells the whole kitchen, not just the oven.** Nvidia sells full supercomputers (DGX), the networking gear that wires thousands of chips together (it bought Mellanox for \$6.9B in 2020), and rents compute through its own cloud.

And the plot twist: on November 12, Jensen Huang, the leather-jacket co-founder who ran it for 33 years, resigned as CEO. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO while the board searches.

Public (NASDAQ: NVDA). Started at Denny's, ended up deciding who gets to build AI. Grand Slam breakfast indeed.

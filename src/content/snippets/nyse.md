In 1792, 24 stockbrokers met under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street and signed an agreement on how they'd trade with each other.

That two-sentence handshake became the New York Stock Exchange, the most famous building in finance.

Most people think the NYSE is a public institution, like a museum or the Fed. It's not. It's a business, and it's owned by a company called Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), which bought it in 2013. ICE itself has a great origin: founder Jeff Sprecher bought a small Atlanta power-trading exchange for \$1 in 1997 and built it into one of the biggest exchange operators in the world.

How the NYSE makes money:

**Listing fees.** Companies pay to list their stock and pay again every year to stay listed.

**Trading fees.** A tiny charge on trades that happen on its exchanges.

**Market data.** This is the sneaky big one. Banks and trading firms pay a lot for fast, detailed price feeds. Information is the product.

**The bell.** Ringing the opening bell isn't really a business line, but it might be the best marketing in finance. Every CEO wants that photo.

The 2026 twist: the NYSE announced it's building a platform for "tokenized" stocks, digital versions of shares that live on a blockchain, so U.S. stocks could trade 24/7. The 234-year-old exchange is trying to never close.

NYSE Group president Lynn Martin is a regular voice on the future of markets, and the NYSE sponsors TBPN.

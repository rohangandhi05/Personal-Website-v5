If you read the Anduril snippet, you'll notice a pattern. Palantir is also named after something from The Lord of the Rings: the palantíri, the seeing stones that let you watch events far away. Silicon Valley's defense scene is one big Tolkien book club.

Founded in 2003 by a group including Peter Thiel and Alex Karp, with early money from In-Q-Tel, the CIA's venture capital arm. Karp has a PhD in philosophy, runs the company in what can only be described as a permanent state of caffeinated enthusiasm, and does earnings calls like a man delivering a closing argument.

What it actually does: software that pulls a big organization's scattered data into one place and lets people make decisions on it. For militaries and intelligence agencies, that's Gotham. For companies, it's Foundry. And now AIP, which plugs AI models into all of it.

How it makes money: big software contracts, with a twist. Palantir sends its own "forward deployed engineers" to sit inside the customer and set things up, more like a special-ops team than a software vendor.

For years the knock on Palantir was "it's just a government contractor." Not anymore. In the July–September 2026 quarter, U.S. commercial revenue hit \$984 million, up 148%, and passed U.S. government revenue for the first time. Its "Rule of 40" score, a common way to grade software companies on growth plus profit, where 40 is considered great, came in at 158.

That's not passing the test. That's grading your own test and the teacher's.

Public (NASDAQ: PLTR). Started as the CIA's crystal ball, now it's every Fortune 500's.

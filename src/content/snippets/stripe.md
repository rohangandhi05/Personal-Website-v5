You have never visited Stripe's website as a customer. You have paid Stripe anyway. Probably this week.

Every time you buy something online from a company that uses Stripe, the money passes through Stripe for a second, and it keeps a little. The standard U.S. price is 2.9% plus 30 cents per card payment. Big customers negotiate that down, but the idea holds: Stripe is a toll booth on the internet economy, and the internet economy never stops driving.

How big is the road? In 2025 businesses pushed \$1.9 trillion through Stripe, up 34% in a year. That's roughly 1.6% of the entire world's GDP passing through a company most people couldn't describe.

What it sells beyond the toll booth: billing and subscriptions, fraud detection, tax tools, loans to its own merchants, and Atlas, which lets anyone incorporate a U.S. company from their laptop. It also helped incubate Tempo, a blockchain for payments, with crypto firm Paradigm.

The origin story is very Irish. Brothers Patrick and John Collison, from a small village in County Tipperary, sold their first company in their teens and started Stripe in 2010 because accepting a payment online took weeks of paperwork. Their pitch: seven lines of code.

Stripe has stayed private for 16 years and keeps saying it's "robustly profitable" without showing anyone the receipts. In February a tender offer let employees sell shares at a \$159 billion valuation, with buyers including Thrive, Coatue and a16z.

Private. The toll booth does not need to go public. The toll booth is doing fine.

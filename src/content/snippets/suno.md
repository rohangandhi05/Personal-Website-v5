Type this into Suno: "a sad country song about my phone dying at 3% while I'm lost in a parking garage."

About a minute later you have a complete song. Lyrics, melody, a singer with a twang, steel guitar, a bridge. It's unsettlingly good, and you will be humming it for the rest of the day.

That's Suno, an AI music company founded in 2022 in Cambridge, Massachusetts, by Mikey Shulman and a few colleagues from an AI startup called Kensho. Its pitch is that anyone can make music, not just people who can play an instrument.

How it makes money: subscriptions. You can make some songs for free, but paying members get more songs, better models and the right to use what they create commercially. It passed 2 million paid subscribers in early 2026, with about \$300 million in annual recurring revenue.

The drama: in 2024 the three biggest record labels, Universal, Sony and Warner, sued Suno, accusing it of training its AI on their copyrighted songs without permission.

Then a plot twist. In November 2025 Warner settled and became a partner instead. Under the deal, Suno agreed to replace its original models with new ones built on licensed music, and it even bought Songkick, the concert-finding app, from Warner. The new licensed models launched in September 2026. Other label lawsuits have continued.

In June 2026 Suno raised \$400 million at a \$5.4 billion valuation, more than double its value from six months before.

Private. The record labels went from "see you in court" to "let's jam." Mostly.

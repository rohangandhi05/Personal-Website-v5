Most security cameras in offices and schools used to be clunky: wired into a recorder in a closet, footage you could only watch on a dusty monitor in the security room, software that looked like Windows 98.

Verkada's idea, back in 2016: what if security cameras worked like an iPhone app? Plug in the camera, and everything lives in the cloud. Search footage from your laptop. Get an alert on your phone. Find "a person in a red shirt" across 50 buildings in seconds.

It was founded by Stanford grads Filip Kaliszan, Hans Robertson, Benjamin Bercovitz and James Ren, and now sells cameras, door access systems, alarms, air quality sensors and more to schools, hospitals, offices and cities.

How it makes money: **hardware plus a required software license.** You buy the camera, then pay for a cloud license for each device, every year. If you stop paying, you lose the cloud features. That gives Verkada recurring software revenue on top of every hardware sale. It said it passed \$1 billion in annualized bookings by the end of 2025.

The low point: in March 2021, hackers got into Verkada's systems and accessed live feeds from around 150,000 cameras, including inside hospitals, jails, schools and Tesla facilities. It was a security company getting its own security very wrong. In 2024 the FTC fined it nearly \$3 million.

Verkada rebuilt trust and kept growing. In December 2025 it was valued at \$5.8 billion, and it's rumored to be eyeing an IPO.

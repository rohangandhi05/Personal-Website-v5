In the summer of 2005, a programmer and essayist named Paul Graham, his future wife Jessica Livingston, and friends Robert Morris and Trevor Blackwell ran an experiment in Cambridge, Massachusetts. They'd give a group of young founders a little money and a lot of advice for one summer and see what happened.

That first batch included two kids from the University of Virginia building a website called Reddit, and a young Stanford dropout named Sam Altman. Not a bad first class.

That was Y Combinator. Its alumni list now reads like a list of every app on your phone: Airbnb, Stripe, DoorDash, Coinbase, Dropbox, Instacart, Reddit, Twitch. Plus plenty of companies in this series, like Flock Safety and Brex.

How it works: four times a year, YC picks a batch of startups. Getting in is reportedly harder than getting into Harvard, with acceptance rates estimated around 1% to 2%. Each company gets \$500,000: \$125,000 for 7% of the company, plus \$375,000 on flexible terms. Then three months of intense advice, ending in Demo Day, where founders pitch a room full of investors.

How YC makes money: equity. It owns small stakes in thousands of companies. Most fail. A few become Airbnb. The few pay for everything many times over. It's venture capital's purest version of the lottery, except YC buys almost every ticket.

Garry Tan, a YC alum himself, now runs it.

Private. Make something people want, as the YC T-shirt says.

Before Zipline was dropping off your Walmart order, it was dropping blood out of the sky in Rwanda.

2016. Hospitals in rural Rwanda kept running out of blood, and the roads made restocking slow. Zipline built a small fixed-wing drone, launched it off a catapult, and had it fly to the hospital and drop a box of blood on a paper parachute. No landing. Just yeet, and the nurse gets a text five minutes before it arrives.

By 2018 it was reportedly flying a big share of the country's blood supply. Then Ghana. Then vaccines, medicine, more countries. Rocky training in the meat locker before the big fight.

Now it's in the American suburbs. The newer drone, P2, hovers high above your yard and lowers a little delivery pod on a tether, so your burrito descends from the heavens with the dignity of Mission Impossible.

How it makes money: it charges partners per delivery. Governments and health systems pay to move medical supplies. Retailers and restaurants like Walmart pay to get orders to your door faster than a driver can find parking.

The big moves this month: a \$1.5 billion raise at a \$21.5 billion valuation (nearly triple its value in January), led by Paradigm, WCM and Layer Global. Walmart is adding 300 drone sites on top of the 21 the two run together today. And Uber is in on it, targeting a million Zipline deliveries a day by the end of 2029.

Private. Co-founder and CEO Keller Rinaudo Cliffton. Started by saving lives, scaling on burritos. Honestly, both are life-saving.
